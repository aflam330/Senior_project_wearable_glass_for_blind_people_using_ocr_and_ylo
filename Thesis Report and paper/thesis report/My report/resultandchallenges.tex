\section{Performance Evaluation Overview}

The evaluation of Savior Glass covers Taka detection, counterfeit authentication on known and on unseen prints, the watermark check, the safety of the spoken verdict, emotion recognition, text recognition, object detection, speed on the Raspberry Pi~5, and a first field test. Each module was evaluated on held-out data that was not used for training or model selection, using the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. Evaluations were run on an NVIDIA RTX~3050 Laptop GPU; device figures were measured on a Raspberry Pi~5 with 8~GB of memory. Every number in this chapter was copied from a saved result file of the project. When a measurement does not exist, it is written as \textit{not measured} rather than estimated. Several figures differ from an earlier draft of this thesis; Section~\ref{sec:corrections} lists the corrections.

\section{Taka Detection Performance}

\subsection{Training Behaviour}
The YOLOv8s detector was fine-tuned from COCO weights for 80 epochs at 640$\times$640 pixels with batch size 16, SGD (initial learning rate 0.01, momentum 0.937, weight decay 0.0005, three warm-up epochs), mosaic 1.0 and mixup 0.15. Horizontal and vertical flips were disabled because a mirrored note is not a real note. Training converged quickly: mAP@0.5 was above 0.97 after 8 epochs and above 0.99 after 11 epochs, while mAP@0.5:0.95 kept improving slowly as the boxes became tighter (Figure~\ref{fig:traincurves}).

\subsection{Unknown Notes}
The detector has no ``unknown note'' output, and it named 44 of the 101 one-Taka photographs as a known denomination (43.6\%). A simple rule reduces this: a denomination is announced only when the confidence is at least 0.60, a value chosen on validation data. On the Bangla Money test photographs the share of 1 Taka notes named as another value falls from 43.6\% to 22.8\%. The rule is used in the glass; about one unknown note in five is still named wrongly.

\subsection{Fall-Back Classifier}
The MobileNetV3-Small classifier used as a fall-back reached a best validation accuracy of 99.2\% on the nine-class BanglaTaka crops. This is a whole-note classification score on clean crops, not a detection score in a scene.

\subsection{Comparison with Related Work}
Compared with published detectors (Table~\ref{tab:currency_compare}), our mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.849 on composites is higher than the 0.804 reported for a custom-head YOLOv8n multi-currency model \cite{yolov8nse}, and our precision and recall (0.995/0.996) are higher than the 0.95/0.93 of the YOLOv5 baseline trained on 3,111 manually annotated images \cite{nstubdtaka2024}. Our labelled dataset is about seven times larger and required no manual annotation. However, the test sets are different and our headline scores are on synthetic images, so the comparison indicates competitiveness, not superiority on a common benchmark.

\subsection{Accuracy for One to Six Views}

The baseline is much weaker with one view (73.6\%) than with six (91.8\%). An earlier version of our fusion model trained only on six views showed the same problem even more strongly: 96.6\% with six views but only 68.8\% with one view. This is the view-count distribution shift described in Section~\ref{sec:method}. The prefix-robust protocol removes it: PRMVT stays between 97.1\% and 98.6\% for every number of views. The difference to the baseline is statistically significant under McNemar's test at one view ($p = 4.3\times10^{-11}$) and at six views ($p = 0.0020$). After a Bonferroni correction over 36 algorithm-by-view comparisons, the one-, two-, three- and five-view gains remain significant, while the four- and six-view gains do not; this correction is reported so that the six-view improvement is not overstated. The PRMVT numbers in this chapter were re-evaluated after a numerical fault was fixed: the entropy of a fully confident prediction was computed as $0 \cdot \log 0$, which is undefined, and the model then reported a probability of 0.5 for that note. The fault affected 23 of the 208 test notes at six views and made the earlier figures slightly lower (six-view accuracy 97.6\% instead of 98.1\%).

\subsection{Controlled Comparison: What Causes the Gain}
PRMVT differs from the baseline in several ways at once (fusion head, batch normalisation per view count, auxiliary losses, prefix sampling). To isolate the cause, the same network was trained four ways, with fixed six-view or prefix sampling and with shared or per-view-count batch normalisation, on three seeds each (Table~\ref{tab:same_arch}).

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering
\caption{Same network, four trainings: test accuracy (\%) on 208 notes, mean $\pm$ standard deviation over seeds 42--44}
\label{tab:same_arch}
\begin{tabular}{|p{5.2cm}|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{Training} & \textbf{1 view} & \textbf{2 views} & \textbf{4 views} & \textbf{6 views} \\ \hline
Fixed 6 views, shared BN & 71.2 $\pm$ 11.4 & 73.1 $\pm$ 17.1 & 90.5 $\pm$ 2.2 & 98.1 $\pm$ 0.5 \\ \hline
\textbf{Prefix, shared BN} & \textbf{98.2 $\pm$ 0.7} & \textbf{98.4 $\pm$ 0.3} & \textbf{98.6 $\pm$ 0.5} & \textbf{98.7 $\pm$ 0.3} \\ \hline
Fixed 6 views, per-count BN & 87.2 $\pm$ 9.7 & 92.8 $\pm$ 6.3 & 95.7 $\pm$ 4.6 & 99.0 $\pm$ 0.0 \\ \hline
Prefix, per-count BN (PRMVT) & 96.5 $\pm$ 1.5 & 98.4 $\pm$ 0.6 & 97.6 $\pm$ 0.8 & 98.1 $\pm$ 1.0 \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Prefix sampling alone is the cause. With shared batch normalisation it beats fixed-view training at one view on every seed (exact McNemar $p = 5.4\times10^{-20}$, $2.1\times10^{-22}$ and $9.3\times10^{-10}$), and there is no difference at six views ($p \geq 0.22$). Fixed-view training is also unstable: its number of correct notes at one view ranges from 131 to 194 across seeds, while prefix training stays between 197 and 206. The reason is that six-view training never constrains what the network does with one view, so the result depends on the random initialisation. The simplest variant, prefix sampling with shared batch normalisation, is the most accurate and was chosen on the validation split as the recommended model.

The effect was also tested outside currency. On MVP-N \cite{mvpn} with an attention-fusion head, prefix training raises one-view accuracy from 40.7\% to 48.0\% and six-view accuracy from 75.8\% to 82.2\%; with a concatenation head it does not help at one view (48.7\% against 49.7\%) and costs 8.6 points at six views. On ten rendered ModelNet classes \cite{modelnet} both trainings give 64\% at one view and 77\% at six. The benefit therefore depends on the fusion head and is not universal.

\subsection{Comparison with Standard Backbones}
Eighteen training variants were run with three seeds each; none beats the shared-BN prefix network. More importantly, frozen ImageNet backbones with a linear classifier per view reach the same level on this split: ResNet-50 \cite{resnet} 98.1\% at one view and 99.0\% at six, DenseNet-121 97.6\% and 99.0\%, MobileNetV2 96.6\% and 98.6\%. In 60 paired McNemar tests between these probes and our networks, no difference was significant. \textbf{No state-of-the-art claim is made on JaalTaka.} The contribution on this split is the explanation and removal of the view-count collapse of jointly trained fusion networks, not a higher number. That a frozen general-purpose backbone reaches 98--99\% is itself a sign that the split is easy, which the next section explains.

\subsection{Repeatability over Seeds}
PRMVT was trained with three random seeds (42, 43, 44). The mean accuracy was 96.5\% (standard deviation 1.6 points) with one view and 98.1\% (standard deviation 1.0 point) with six views; the lowest mean over the six view counts was 96.5\% (Table~\ref{tab:multiseed}). The result is therefore not a lucky seed, although three seeds is a small sample.

\subsection{Other Metrics and Calibration}
For the six-view operating point of the fixed-count fusion model, the improvement over the baseline is consistent across metrics: accuracy 0.966 vs.\ 0.918, macro-F1 0.966 vs.\ 0.914, balanced accuracy 0.965 vs.\ 0.906, ROC-AUC 0.989 vs.\ 0.971, and expected calibration error (ECE) 0.030 vs.\ 0.079. A lower ECE means that the confidence reported by the model matches its real accuracy better, which is important if the glass tells the user ``probably counterfeit''. For PRMVT, the six-view ECE without any calibration is 0.015, so its confidence is already well matched to its accuracy; calibration maps fitted on the validation set (temperature scaling and a quality-aware map) do not improve on it (0.017--0.018). An earlier uncalibrated value of 0.061 was caused by the numerical fault described above, which reported a probability of 0.5 for notes the model was certain about. The confusion matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:auth_confusion}) show that PRMVT mainly removes the baseline's errors on counterfeit notes that were accepted as genuine (a fraction of 0.07 of all test notes for the baseline, 0.01 for PRMVT).

\subsection{Robustness to Image Corruptions}
The models were tested under 13 image corruptions at several strengths (Figure~\ref{fig:auth_robust}). PRMVT is almost unaffected by Gaussian and motion blur, JPEG compression, scale change and sensor noise. It is clearly sensitive to low light, strong brightness, rotation, perspective and especially occlusion: at an occlusion of 55\% of the image, six-view accuracy fell to 51.9\%, which is worse than the baseline (81.3\%). Fine-tuning with low-light augmentation raised accuracy at the darkest setting (brightness factor 0.2) to 93.75\% while keeping 98.6\% on clean images, but a first, short occlusion fine-tune did not fix occlusion (68.3\%, with clean accuracy falling to 90.9\%). An analysis on the validation set showed why: under occlusion PRMVT called most genuine notes counterfeit, because a covered region looks like a missing security feature, and the earlier fine-tunes had kept the CNN frozen and trained only briefly. A longer fine-tune in which every view is randomly covered and the last CNN blocks can adapt, with the epoch chosen on the validation set, raised accuracy at 55\% occlusion to 88.5\% (87.0\% $\pm$ 2.2 over three seeds, 88.9\% for their average) while clean accuracy stayed at 98--99\%. This is still below 90\%. If the model is allowed to answer only when it is at least 99\% confident and otherwise asks the user to reposition the note, it answers about half of the occluded notes with 99.0\% accuracy, and wrong verdicts fall from 11.1\% to 0.5\% of occluded notes. These tests use a black rectangle; a real finger is neither black nor rectangular, so occlusion by fingers remains the main open failure mode.

\section{Counterfeit Detection on Unseen Prints}
\label{sec:unseen}
The serial-number audit (Section~\ref{sec:serial_audit}) showed that most test counterfeits of the note-disjoint split are copies of prints that are also in the training data. Three checks measure how much this matters.

\textbf{Seen and unseen serials.} On the note-disjoint test split, PRMVT with one view catches 67 of the 68 counterfeits whose serial occurs in training (98.5\%) and 17 of the 19 whose serial does not (89.5\%). With 19 notes this is suggestive, not proof.

\textbf{Masking the serial.} When the two serial boxes are covered at test time, accuracy falls by 3 to 5 points (one view: 97.1\% to 92.3\%), and genuine notes lose about as much as counterfeits. Counterfeits with a seen serial are still caught (65 to 68 of 69). The model is therefore not simply reading a memorised serial; it has learned the look of the known prints.

\textbf{Print-disjoint split.} The decisive test trains and tests on the split of Section~\ref{sec:serial_split}, in which every test counterfeit comes from a print never seen in training (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).

Four findings follow.
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Accuracy falls by about 8 points.} The prefix network scores 98.2--98.7\% on the note-disjoint split and 89.3--89.9\% on unseen prints. The frozen ResNet-50 falls from 98--99\% to 84--85\%.
    \item \textbf{The watermark recovers about 5 points.} Combining the network score with the watermark score, with a combiner fitted on validation data, gives 94.4\% at one view and 95.0\% at six, with almost no variation across seeds. The watermark is a physical property of a genuine note and does not depend on which print a counterfeit comes from.
    \item \textbf{A properly fine-tuned ResNet-50 is as good.} A first fine-tuned ResNet-50 baseline scored 89.9\% at six views, and the hybrid beat it. That baseline had been trained on views decoded at a quarter of their resolution because of a fault in an image cache. Trained on full-resolution views it reaches 94.4--94.9\%, level with the hybrid. Per seed, the exact McNemar test between the hybrid and the quarter-resolution baseline at six views gives $p = 2.7\times10^{-5}$, 0.035 and 0.51; against the full-resolution baseline the hybrid is not better, and adding that baseline to the hybrid does not help (94.6\%). We therefore claim a tie, not a win. The two differ in their errors: at six views the hybrid misses 7 of 101 counterfeits on every seed and calls 4 of 121 genuine notes counterfeit, while the fine-tuned ResNet-50 misses 7, 9 and 18 and calls 1 genuine note counterfeit. The hybrid is the more stable of the two.
    \item \textbf{A serial blacklist is useless against new prints.} It catches none of the unseen-serial counterfeits, by construction.
\end{enumerate}
The test split contains about 24 distinct counterfeit prints. At the level of prints, the uncertainty of every figure in Table~\ref{tab:unseen} is roughly $\pm$12 points, so differences of a few points between methods cannot be established with this dataset.

Fine-tuning the authenticator on synthetic whole notes looked better on synthetic test data (accuracy 87.3\% against 79.0\%) but worse on real photographs (AUC 0.689), and at its own validation threshold it passed 14 of 20 real counterfeit photographs as genuine. Under the rule fixed beforehand it was not deployed.

\subsection{Other Training Variants and Negative Results}
Many other training variants were tried on the same split (Figure~\ref{fig:auth_views}); eighteen methods have all three seeds. Several of them reach 95--98\% at one view, for example a focal-style loss variant (NDAL) \cite{focalloss} with 98.1\% at one view, so the accuracy alone does not single out one algorithm; the common factor is that all of them see varying numbers of views during training. Some variants failed: a simulated federated version stayed at 74.0\% with one view and below the baseline from two views on, and one retrained variant became worse than its first version. We also tried learned policies that choose which view to capture next and when to stop (rules based on image quality, confidence, diversity and uncertainty, and a cost-aware rule). On PRMVT, averaged over three seeds, none of them beat simply taking the views in their fixed order (96.5\% with one view and 97.6--98.6\% with two to six): the learned rules picked a weaker first view and reached 89.3--90.2\% with one view, and the cost-aware rule with early stopping stopped after one view at 89.3\%. A prefix stopping rule on the fixed order reached 97.1\% with an average of 1.01 views, which is simply because PRMVT is already accurate with one view. Earlier, much lower figures for these policies (down to 42\%) were caused by evaluation faults that have since been fixed, not by the policies themselves. Values from a single seed also shrank when more seeds were added (one variant fell from 98.1\% to 96.8\% at one view), and about 110 training runs have read this test split, which is a further reason not to rank methods by small differences on it. These negative results are reported so that the method is not presented as better than it is.

\subsection{Speed}
On the RTX~3050 laptop GPU, one six-view authentication takes a median of 42.6~ms (95th percentile 69.5~ms). On the Raspberry Pi~5 it takes 37~ms with one view and 90~ms with six (Section~\ref{sec:pi5}).

\section*{Calculation Methods and Full Working}

This section presents the formulas used in this chapter and a worked example for each main table.

\subsection*{1) Detection Precision, Recall and mAP (Table~\ref{tab:det_overall})}
With $TP$, $FP$ and $FN$ the numbers of true positives, false positives and false negatives at a given IoU threshold,
\[
\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \qquad \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}, \qquad
\text{IoU}(A,B) = \frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}.
\]
The average precision (AP) of one class is the area under its precision--recall curve; mAP@0.5 is the mean AP over the 9 classes at IoU $\geq 0.5$, and mAP@0.5:0.95 is the mean over the ten IoU thresholds $0.50, 0.55, \dots, 0.95$.

\subsection*{2) Raw-Photograph Detection Rate and Top-1 Accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:wild})}
\[
\text{Detection rate} = \frac{\text{notes with at least one box}}{\text{all notes}} = \frac{450-7}{450} = \frac{443}{450} = 98.4\%,
\]
\[
\text{Top-1 accuracy} = \frac{\text{notes with correct top box}}{\text{all notes}} = \frac{436}{450} = 96.9\%,
\]
where $436 = 48+48+47+47+50+50+50+46+50$ is the sum of the correct notes over the nine denominations.

\subsection*{3) Authentication Accuracy and McNemar's Test (Table~\ref{tab:auth_views})}
With 208 test notes, one-view PRMVT accuracy $= 202/208 = 97.12\%$ and baseline accuracy $= 153/208 = 73.56\%$. McNemar's test uses only the discordant notes: $n_{01}$ (baseline correct, PRMVT wrong) and $n_{10}$ (baseline wrong, PRMVT correct):
\[
\chi^2 = \frac{(|n_{01}-n_{10}|-1)^2}{n_{01}+n_{10}},
\]
with one degree of freedom. For example, for the fixed-count fusion model at six views $n_{01}=3$ and $n_{10}=13$, giving $\chi^2 = 81/16 = 5.06$ and $p = 0.024$.

\subsection*{4) Expected Calibration Error}
Predictions are grouped into $M = 10$ confidence bins $B_m$:
\[
\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\bigl|\text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m)\bigr|.
\]

\subsection*{5) Emotion Macro-F1 (Table~\ref{tab:emotion_perclass})}
For each class $c$, $F1_c = 2P_cR_c/(P_c+R_c)$. For fear, $F1 = 2\times0.774\times0.554/(0.774+0.554) = 0.646$. Macro-F1 is the unweighted mean of the seven class F1 scores: $(0.936+0.839+0.799+0.870+0.646+0.627+0.851)/7 = 0.795$.

\subsection*{6) OCR Error Rates (Table~\ref{tab:ocr})}
\[
\text{CER} = \frac{S+D+I}{N},
\]
where $S$, $D$ and $I$ are the character substitutions, deletions and insertions needed to turn the recognised text into the ground truth, and $N$ is the number of ground-truth characters. WER is computed in the same way on words. Because insertions are counted, the rate can exceed 100\% when an engine returns much more text than the image contains, as for the ByT5 corrector in Table~\ref{tab:ocr_steps}.

\subsection*{7) Wilson Interval and the Safe Threshold (Table~\ref{tab:safe})}
For $x$ events in $n$ trials with $\hat{p} = x/n$ and $z = 1.96$, the Wilson interval is
\[
\frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}.
\]
For 0 counterfeits passed out of 88, the upper limit is $\frac{z^2/n}{1+z^2/n} = \frac{3.84/88}{1+3.84/88} = 4.2\%$. The threshold $\tau$ is the highest of the $n = 88$ validation counterfeit scores, so a new exchangeable counterfeit exceeds it with probability $1/(n+1) = 1/89 = 1.1\%$.


\section{Discussion}
The results answer the research questions of Section~\ref{sec:Research Question}.

\textbf{System (RQ1).} A five-mode assistive glass runs on a Raspberry Pi~5 within 1.5~GB of memory and without thermal throttling. Currency, objects, the watermark check and emotion respond in 50 to 330~ms; with speech, a currency answer takes under three seconds. Text reading does not fit: 19~seconds per capture is too slow for comfortable use, and the text detector is the bottleneck.

\textbf{Synthetic data for currency (RQ2).} Training only on automatically labelled composites gave near-perfect scores on held-out composites and 91.5\% correct denominations on 1,536 independently collected photographs, though only 18.5\% on hand-held close-ups. Realistic backgrounds and physically motivated augmentation mattered more than the network architecture. The detector's behaviour through the glass camera is not yet known.

\textbf{Number of views (RQ3).} A model trained with a fixed number of views fails when fewer are available, and unpredictably so across seeds; training on random view prefixes fixes this and keeps accuracy at about 98\% from one to six views. The controlled comparison shows that prefix sampling alone is responsible. The benefit depends on the fusion head and did not appear on every other dataset.

\textbf{Unseen prints (RQ4).} The usual JaalTaka split is a test of known reprints: a serial lookup scores 90.2\% on it, and frozen general-purpose backbones reach 98--99\%. On unseen prints accuracy falls by about 8 points. The watermark recovers about 5 of them and makes the result stable across seeds, but a properly fine-tuned ResNet-50 does as well. With about 24 test prints, the dataset cannot separate the two.

\textbf{Safety (RQ5).} A policy that never says ``counterfeit'' and confirms a note only above the highest validation counterfeit score passed none of 88 test counterfeits. It is useful on close-up views (57.5\% of genuine notes confirmed) but nearly silent on whole notes (0.4--2\%). Safety was achieved; usefulness on whole notes was not.

\textbf{Bangla OCR (RQ6).} Region selection matters most: choosing the main text block removed three quarters of the error. Parameter tuning and clean-up rules added small gains; pre-processing beyond contrast enhancement, other engines, other detectors, dictionaries, language models and multi-scale reading did not help. Fine-tuning the recogniser is promising but unproven.

\textbf{Interaction (RQ7).} Separating capture from reading, cooldowns, queue clearing on mode change, mixed-script speech, immediate mode names and spoken capture guidance were designed for users without sight. Whether they help can only be shown with a user study, which has not been done.

\textbf{A general lesson.} The largest corrections in this project came from looking at the data and the test protocol and not from new models: the serial numbers on the counterfeit notes, the shape of the rendered Bangla text, the resolution of a cached image, the source of a ``wild'' test set. Each of these had produced a good-looking number that was wrong or meant something else.

\section{Limitations}
The following limitations must be considered when interpreting the results:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{The worn glass is not validated.} In the first field test currency recognition was unreliable and Bangla text was not read, and the cause is not established. No accuracy has been measured on photographs taken through the glass camera.
    \item \textbf{No user study.} The system has not been tested with visually impaired participants; usability claims are design intentions, not measured outcomes.
    \item \textbf{Few counterfeit prints.} The unseen-print results rest on about 24 distinct counterfeit prints, with an uncertainty of about $\pm$12 points at the level of prints, and on one currency.
    \item \textbf{Whole-note counterfeit checking is safe but not useful.} The policy confirms 0.4--2\% of genuine whole notes from independent photographs, and its safety on whole notes rests on about four physical counterfeit notes.
    \item \textbf{Synthetic OCR benchmark.} The OCR figures come from rendered text, some of it placed in real photographs. Real signs, glare and curved packets will be harder.
    \item \textbf{Slow text reading on the Pi.} About 19~seconds per capture.
    \item \textbf{Hand-held close-ups and unknown notes.} The detector scores 18.5\% on hand-held close-ups and still names about one unknown note in five as a known denomination.
    \item \textbf{Single capture collection for authentication.} JaalTaka has no camera or session identifiers, so generalisation to other cameras and lighting is untested. The authenticator is a visible-light aid, not a substitute for bank equipment.
    \item \textbf{Capture-guide limits from another camera.} The brightness and sharpness limits were set on JaalTaka photographs.
    \item \textbf{Occlusion.} Authentication degrades when the note is partly covered: 88.5\% at 55\% synthetic occlusion after occlusion fine-tuning, and occlusion by real fingers has not been measured.
    \item \textbf{Generic objects.} The object mode uses the 80 COCO classes, which do not include many objects important for navigation (stairs, open drains, rickshaws), and gives no distance.
    \item \textbf{Offline Bangla voice.} Bangla is spoken with a formant synthesiser on the Pi; the neural Bangla voice could not be used.
    \item \textbf{Not measured.} Battery life; the online mode; the emotion mode and OCR pipeline v2 on the Pi.
\end{enumerate}
