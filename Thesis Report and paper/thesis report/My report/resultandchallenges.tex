\section{Performance Evaluation Overview}

The evaluation of SARA covers Taka detection, counterfeit authentication on known and on unseen prints, the watermark check, the safety of the spoken verdict, emotion recognition, text recognition, object detection, speed on the Raspberry Pi~5, and a first field test. Each module was evaluated on held-out data that was not used for training or model selection, using the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. Evaluations were run on an NVIDIA RTX~3050 Laptop GPU; device figures were measured on a Raspberry Pi~5 with 8~GB of memory. Every number in this chapter was copied from a saved result file of the project. When a measurement does not exist, it is written as \textit{not measured} rather than estimated. Several figures differ from an earlier draft of this thesis; Section~\ref{sec:corrections} lists the corrections.

\section{Taka Detection Performance}

\subsection{Training Behaviour}
The YOLOv8s detector was fine-tuned from COCO weights for 80 epochs at 640$\times$640 pixels with batch size 16, SGD (initial learning rate 0.01, momentum 0.937, weight decay 0.0005, three warm-up epochs), mosaic 1.0 and mixup 0.15. Horizontal and vertical flips were disabled because a mirrored note is not a real note. Training converged quickly: mAP@0.5 was above 0.97 after 8 epochs and above 0.99 after 11 epochs, while mAP@0.5:0.95 kept improving slowly as the boxes became tighter (Figure~\ref{fig:traincurves}).

\subsection{Hand-Held Notes: Fine-Tuning with Synthetic Close-Ups}
\label{sec:handheld}
A blind user holds the note close to the camera, often folded and partly under the fingers, and the training composites showed whole notes inside scenes. Six thousand hand-held close-ups were therefore generated from the note photographs of the training split only (Figure~\ref{fig:closeups}): the note warped, turned by any angle, folded in 40\% of the pictures, scaled to fill or overflow the frame, with one to three finger shapes in skin tones across it, motion blur, low resolution, and colour and compression changes. No NSTU photograph was used to make them. The deployed detector was fine-tuned for 12 epochs on the original training pictures plus the close-ups.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig58_handheld_closeups.png}
    \caption{Synthetic hand-held close-ups used to fine-tune the Taka detector: notes overflowing the frame, folded, turned, blurred and crossed by finger shapes}

\subsection{Unknown Notes}
The detector has no ``unknown note'' output, and it named 44 of the 101 one-Taka photographs as a known denomination (43.6\%). A simple rule reduces this: a denomination is announced only when the confidence is at least 0.60, a value chosen on validation data. On the Bangla Money test photographs the share of 1 Taka notes named as another value falls from 43.6\% to 22.8\%. The rule is used in the glass; about one unknown note in five is still named wrongly.

\subsection{Live Check with a Laptop Webcam}
The detector was also run live on a laptop webcam with real notes held in the hand (Figure~\ref{fig:live_webcam}). The application draws a box with the denomination and the confidence on the camera image and speaks the note when the space bar is pressed. In these sessions 10, 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1,000 Taka notes were named correctly at confidences between 0.63 and 0.89, including worn and folded notes, notes held at an angle, and two or three notes in one picture. The pictures also show the detector's weak points. The boxes are loose: they include the hand and part of the background. When notes are close together the boxes overlap, and in the last picture the 500 Taka box also covers the 200 Taka note. These pictures are illustrations from informal sessions. \subsection{Several Notes in One Picture}
The count and the spoken total were measured on 900 pictures built for this test: one, two or three notes of the detector's test split on backgrounds that were not used in training, with the training generator's tilt, rotation, colour, blur and compression, and notes that overlap by at most 5\%. The counting rule has no tuned number. The count was right in 99.7\% of the pictures and every note, and so the total, in 99.2\% (one note: 299 of 300; two notes: 298 of 300; three notes: 296 of 300). No note was counted twice. These are synthetic pictures on the laptop; a hand holding a fan of overlapping notes will be harder.

\subsection{Fall-Back Classifier}
The MobileNetV3-Small classifier used as a fall-back reached a best validation accuracy of 99.2\% on the nine-class BanglaTaka crops. This is a whole-note classification score on clean crops, not a detection score in a scene.

\subsection{Comparison with Related Work}
Compared with published detectors (Table~\ref{tab:currency_compare}), our mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.849 on composites is higher than the 0.804 reported for a custom-head YOLOv8n multi-currency model \cite{yolov8nse}, and our precision and recall (0.995/0.996) are higher than the 0.95/0.93 of the YOLOv5 baseline trained on 3,111 manually annotated images \cite{nstubdtaka2024}. Our labelled dataset is about seven times larger and required no manual annotation. However, the test sets are different and our headline scores are on synthetic images, so the comparison indicates competitiveness, not superiority on a common benchmark.

The baseline is much weaker with one view (73.6\%) than with six (91.8\%). An earlier version of our fusion model trained only on six views showed the same problem even more strongly: 96.6\% with six views but only 68.8\% with one view. The fault affected 23 of the 208 test notes at six views and made the earlier figures slightly lower (six-view accuracy 97.6\% instead of 98.1\%).

Prefix sampling alone is the cause. With shared batch normalisation it beats fixed-view training at one view on every seed (exact McNemar $p = 5.4\times10^{-20}$, $2.1\times10^{-22}$ and $9.3\times10^{-10}$), and there is no difference at six views ($p \geq 0.22$). Fixed-view training is also unstable: its number of correct notes at one view ranges from 131 to 194 across seeds, while prefix training stays between 197 and 206. The reason is that six-view training never constrains what the network does with one view, so the result depends on the random initialisation. The simplest variant, prefix sampling with shared batch normalisation, is the most accurate and was chosen on the validation split as the recommended model.

The effect was also tested outside currency. On MVP-N \cite{mvpn} with an attention-fusion head, prefix training raises one-view accuracy from 40.7\% to 48.0\% and six-view accuracy from 75.8\% to 82.2\%; with a concatenation head it does not help at one view (48.7\% against 49.7\%) and costs 8.6 points at six views. On ten rendered ModelNet classes \cite{modelnet} both trainings give 64\% at one view and 77\% at six. The benefit therefore depends on the fusion head and is not universal.

\subsection{Comparison with Standard Backbones}
Eighteen training variants were run with three seeds each; none beats the shared-BN prefix network. More importantly, frozen ImageNet backbones with a linear classifier per view reach the same level on this split: ResNet-50 \cite{resnet} 98.1\% at one view and 99.0\% at six, DenseNet-121 97.6\% and 99.0\%, MobileNetV2 96.6\% and 98.6\%. In 60 paired McNemar tests between these probes and our networks, no difference was significant. \textbf{No state-of-the-art claim is made on JaalTaka.} The contribution on this split is the explanation and removal of the view-count collapse of jointly trained fusion networks, not a higher number. That a frozen general-purpose backbone reaches 98--99\% is itself a sign that the split is easy, which the next section explains.

\subsection{Other Metrics and Calibration}
For the six-view operating point of the fixed-count fusion model, the improvement over the baseline is consistent across metrics: accuracy 0.966 vs.\ 0.918, macro-F1 0.966 vs.\ 0.914, balanced accuracy 0.965 vs.\ 0.906, ROC-AUC 0.989 vs.\ 0.971, and expected calibration error (ECE) 0.030 vs.\ 0.079. A lower ECE means that the confidence reported by the model matches its real accuracy better, which is important if the glass tells the user ``probably counterfeit''. For PRMVT, the six-view ECE without any calibration is 0.015, so its confidence is already well matched to its accuracy; calibration maps fitted on the validation set (temperature scaling and a quality-aware map) do not improve on it (0.017--0.018). An earlier uncalibrated value of 0.061 was caused by the numerical fault described above, which reported a probability of 0.5 for notes the model was certain about. The confusion matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:auth_confusion}) show that PRMVT mainly removes the baseline's errors on counterfeit notes that were accepted as genuine (a fraction of 0.07 of all test notes for the baseline, 0.01 for PRMVT).

\subsection{Robustness to Image Corruptions}
The models were tested under 13 image corruptions at several strengths (Figure~\ref{fig:auth_robust}). PRMVT is almost unaffected by Gaussian and motion blur, JPEG compression, scale change and sensor noise. It is clearly sensitive to low light, strong brightness, rotation, perspective and especially occlusion: at an occlusion of 55\% of the image, six-view accuracy fell to 51.9\%, which is worse than the baseline (81.3\%). Fine-tuning with low-light augmentation raised accuracy at the darkest setting (brightness factor 0.2) to 93.75\% while keeping 98.6\% on clean images, but a first, short occlusion fine-tune did not fix occlusion (68.3\%, with clean accuracy falling to 90.9\%). An analysis on the validation set showed why: under occlusion PRMVT called most genuine notes counterfeit, because a covered region looks like a missing security feature, and the earlier fine-tunes had kept the CNN frozen and trained only briefly. A longer fine-tune in which every view is randomly covered and the last CNN blocks can adapt, with the epoch chosen on the validation set, raised accuracy at 55\% occlusion to 88.5\% (87.0\% $\pm$ 2.2 over three seeds, 88.9\% for their average) while clean accuracy stayed at 98--99\%. This is still below 90\%. If the model is allowed to answer only when it is at least 99\% confident and otherwise asks the user to reposition the note, it answers about half of the occluded notes with 99.0\% accuracy, and wrong verdicts fall from 11.1\% to 0.5\% of occluded notes. These tests use a black rectangle; a real finger is neither black nor rectangular, so occlusion by fingers remains the main open failure mode.

\section{Counterfeit Detection on Unseen Prints}
\label{sec:unseen}
The serial-number audit (Section~\ref{sec:serial_audit}) showed that most test counterfeits of the note-disjoint split are copies of prints that are also in the training data. Three checks measure how much this matters.

\textbf{Seen and unseen serials.} On the note-disjoint test split, PRMVT with one view catches 67 of the 68 counterfeits whose serial occurs in training (98.5\%) and 17 of the 19 whose serial does not (89.5\%). With 19 notes this is suggestive, not proof.

\textbf{Masking the serial.} When the two serial boxes are covered at test time, accuracy falls by 3 to 5 points (one view: 97.1\% to 92.3\%), and genuine notes lose about as much as counterfeits. Counterfeits with a seen serial are still caught (65 to 68 of 69). The model is therefore not simply reading a memorised serial; it has learned the look of the known prints.

\textbf{Print-disjoint split.} The decisive test trains and tests on the split of Section~\ref{sec:serial_split}, in which every test counterfeit comes from a print never seen in training (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).

Four findings follow.
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Accuracy falls by about 8 points.} The prefix network scores 98.2--98.7\% on the note-disjoint split and 89.3--89.9\% on unseen prints. The frozen ResNet-50 falls from 98--99\% to 84--85\%.
    \item \textbf{The watermark recovers about 5 points.} Combining the network score with the watermark score, with a combiner fitted on validation data, gives 94.4\% at one view and 95.0\% at six, with almost no variation across seeds. The watermark is a physical property of a genuine note and does not depend on which print a counterfeit comes from.
    \item \textbf{A properly fine-tuned ResNet-50 is as good.} A first fine-tuned ResNet-50 baseline scored 89.9\% at six views, and the hybrid beat it. That baseline had been trained on views decoded at a quarter of their resolution because of a fault in an image cache. Trained on full-resolution views it reaches 94.4--94.9\%, level with the hybrid. Per seed, the exact McNemar test between the hybrid and the quarter-resolution baseline at six views gives $p = 2.7\times10^{-5}$, 0.035 and 0.51; against the full-resolution baseline the hybrid is not better, and adding that baseline to the hybrid does not help (94.6\%). We therefore claim a tie, not a win. The two differ in their errors: at six views the hybrid misses 7 of 101 counterfeits on every seed and calls 4 of 121 genuine notes counterfeit, while the fine-tuned ResNet-50 misses 7, 9 and 18 and calls 1 genuine note counterfeit. The hybrid is the more stable of the two.
    \item \textbf{A serial blacklist is useless against new prints.} It catches none of the unseen-serial counterfeits, by construction.
\end{enumerate}
The test split contains about 24 distinct counterfeit prints. At the level of prints, the uncertainty of every figure in Table~\ref{tab:unseen} is roughly $\pm$12 points, so differences of a few points between methods cannot be established with this dataset.

\subsection{UV Security-Fibre Check}
\label{sec:uv_results}
The check of Section~\ref{sec:uv_method} was measured on 118 phone photographs of genuine 200, 500 and 1,000 Taka notes under a UV lamp, taken for this project. \subsection{Other Training Variants and Negative Results}
Many other training variants were tried on the same split (Figure~\ref{fig:auth_views}); eighteen methods have all three seeds. Several of them reach 95--98\% at one view, for example a focal-style loss variant (NDAL) \cite{focalloss} with 98.1\% at one view, so the accuracy alone does not single out one algorithm; the common factor is that all of them see varying numbers of views during training. Some variants failed: a simulated federated version stayed at 74.0\% with one view and below the baseline from two views on, and one retrained variant became worse than its first version. We also tried learned policies that choose which view to capture next and when to stop (rules based on image quality, confidence, diversity and uncertainty, and a cost-aware rule). On PRMVT, averaged over three seeds, none of them beat simply taking the views in their fixed order (96.5\% with one view and 97.6--98.6\% with two to six): the learned rules picked a weaker first view and reached 89.3--90.2\% with one view, and the cost-aware rule with early stopping stopped after one view at 89.3\%. A prefix stopping rule on the fixed order reached 97.1\% with an average of 1.01 views, which is simply because PRMVT is already accurate with one view. Earlier, much lower figures for these policies (down to 42\%) were caused by evaluation faults that have since been fixed, not by the policies themselves. Values from a single seed also shrank when more seeds were added (one variant fell from 98.1\% to 96.8\% at one view), and about 110 training runs have read this test split, which is a further reason not to rank methods by small differences on it. These negative results are reported so that the method is not presented as better than it is.

\subsection{Speed}
On the RTX~3050 laptop GPU, one six-view authentication takes a median of 42.6~ms (95th percentile 69.5~ms). On the Raspberry Pi~5 it takes 37~ms with one view and 90~ms with six (Section~\ref{sec:pi5}).

\textbf{Where the errors were.} On photographs of signs the first pipeline had a CER of 45.0\%. Inspection showed that the sign itself was usually read correctly; the errors were text read from the background and words in the wrong order. Selecting the main text block removes both, and photo-scene CER falls to 6.4\%. This single step gives most of the total gain.

\textbf{What did not help.} Binarisation and perspective correction hurt. Tesseract \cite{tesseract} was the best other engine but had about seven times the error; the available TrOCR models \cite{trocr} returned unrelated words for Bangla; PaddleOCR has no Bangla model. A spelling dictionary \cite{symspell} and a character language model \cite{kenlm} both replace correct but uncommon Bangla words by common ones. The ByT5 corrector \cite{byt5} rewrote and extended the text and took several seconds per line. Reading at three scales costs three OCR passes and is not better than one. Replacing the CRAFT detector by DBNet \cite{dbnet} (3.4\%), EAST \cite{east} (18.3\%) or a public YOLO text model (50.6\%) inside the same pipeline was also worse than CRAFT (2.3\%); the YOLO model was trained on handwriting in another script, so its result says nothing about a detector trained for this task.

The benchmark figures do not carry over to real street photographs: the error is more than ten times higher. Much of it is silence. On a quarter of the Bangla words and on half of the Latin-script words nothing was read at all, which points to the text-finding thresholds and the confidence filter, both chosen on rendered text. Words rotated by 90$^\circ$ are not read (84\% and 99\% error), and many Latin-script words are small or are French, German or Italian, for which the recogniser has no model. For orientation, the BanglaWild benchmark reports 48.4\% CER for plain EasyOCR on real Bangla scene photographs \cite{banglawild}; the data differ, so only the order of magnitude can be compared. Reading real signs is therefore the main open problem of the text mode, next to the glass camera.

\textbf{Settings chosen on real words.} Because so many words were left unread, four settings of the text finder and of the confidence filter were compared on a separate real validation sample (150 Bangla and 150 Latin-script words that are not among the 1,000 test words). The rule was fixed beforehand: the lowest error on the real validation words wins, among settings whose error on the rendered validation set stays within 0.5 points. Lowering the confidence filter from 0.2 to 0.1 won (real validation 47.8\% against 51.1\%; rendered validation 2.58\% against 2.14\%). EasyOCR's own detector defaults were worse on both kinds of data. Read once on the 1,000 test words, the new setting lowers the error from 41.7\% to 39.2\% for Bangla and from 64.9\% to 62.5\% for Latin script, and the share of unread words from 24.6\% to 17.0\% and from 48.4\% to 38.0\% (bold rows of Table~\ref{tab:ocr_real}). The gain is real but small: the words that are now read are often read wrongly. On the rendered test set the pipeline as it now is in the glass (greedy decoder, confidence filter 0.1) scores 2.5\% CER (Bangla 3.3\%, English 1.7\%), practically unchanged. Thresholds are now exhausted; the remaining error lies in the recogniser.

\textbf{A recogniser trained on real words.} EasyOCR's Bangla and English recogniser was therefore fine-tuned on real training words from the same collection (none of them among the validation or test words), mixed with rendered words, three times with different seeds. Each model was read through the glass's own pipeline. The fully fine-tuned models lowered the real-validation error from 47.8\% to 34--35\%, but raised the rendered-validation error from 2.58\% to 3.3--3.8\% (printed English to 4.9--5.8\%): they learned the street words and forgot part of the clean printed text, and so failed the 0.5-point rule. Blending the weights half and half with the released ones, $\theta = \tfrac12\theta_{\text{released}} + \tfrac12\theta_{\text{fine-tuned}}$ (a rule added after the first model failed and before any other was scored), kept most of the gain without the loss: real validation 36.7--37.2\%, rendered validation 2.33--2.63\%, all three within the rule. The one with the lowest real-validation error (seed~44) was chosen. A retry with the picture turned by 90$^\circ$ when nothing is read left fewer Latin words unread but did not lower the error, and was not adopted. Read once on the 1,000 test words, the chosen recogniser lowers the error from 39.2\% to \textbf{28.7\%} for Bangla and from 62.5\% to \textbf{50.6\%} for Latin script, and on the rendered test set from 2.46\% to 2.27\% (bold rows of Table~\ref{tab:ocr_real}). It has the same network as the released recogniser, so it costs no time, and it is now the glass's default.

\textbf{How much the picture is enlarged.} The glass enlarges a 640-pixel camera picture to 1,200 pixels before the text detector, whose time grows with the number of pixels. Widths of 1,040, 960 and 880 pixels were scored with the full-precision and the 8-bit detector. The validation rule chose 880 for both, but the single test read did not confirm it (rendered test error 3.15\% and 3.12\% against 2.46\% at 1,200, printed Bangla 4.9\%), and the validation error went up and down with the width in no order. The width therefore stays at 1,200; 880 remains a faster option whose speed on the Pi is still to be measured. This is the one place where a rule fixed beforehand was not followed, and the departure is towards the more accurate setting.

\subsection{Handwritten Bangla Character Recognition}
The CNN trained on Ekush reached 90.0\% test accuracy on 38,608 characters from 305 unseen writers (best validation accuracy 91.1\%). Accuracy was 94.3\% for digits, 94.2\% for modifiers, 93.1\% for vowels, 89.9\% for consonants and 87.7\% for conjuncts. The lower accuracy for conjuncts confirms that compound characters are the hardest part of Bangla recognition, which is also the main source of the OCR errors above.

\subsection{Heat on the Pi and a Thermal Governor}
\label{sec:thermal}
The board's own log of the second run (one sample every 2~s, 651 samples) explains the throttling. While idle before the run the board was already at 71~$^\circ$C with the fan at 7,800~rpm; during object scans it averaged 84~$^\circ$C and during text reading 85~$^\circ$C (CPU 95--99\% busy, peak 88.7~$^\circ$C). A first-order fit of the log, $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}t = (T_\infty - T)/\tau$, gives $\tau \approx 7$~s and $T_\infty \approx 74.8 + 0.105\,u$ for a CPU load of $u$\%: with this cooling, an average load above about 50\% keeps the board above 80~$^\circ$C, where the firmware lowers the clock. The idle temperature points first to the cooling (room temperature, the seating of the cooler or a closed case), which software cannot change.

Software can lower the load that nobody asked for at that moment. A thermal governor reads the temperature once a second and the firmware's throttle flags every 5~s, and slows the automatic object scan from every 1.5~s to every 3~s above 72~$^\circ$C and every 6~s above 77~$^\circ$C (or when the firmware's soft limit is active), and pauses it above 81~$^\circ$C; the live preview boxes stop above 77~$^\circ$C. A button press or a voice command is never delayed. The states have hysteresis, single hot samples are smoothed, and every change is written to the field log. Whether this keeps the board below 80~$^\circ$C in use has not been measured; a power logger that sums the Pi~5's own supply rails (\texttt{vcgencmd pmic\_read\_adc}) every 2~s and estimates battery life was added for that run.

\subsection{Voice Output and Voice Commands (14 October 2026)}
\label{sec:voice_results}
\textbf{Voice output.} After the second Pi run the voice was reported as broken up. Two causes were found in the code. First, speech was synthesised and played at the same time, so the player ran out of samples whenever text reading held all four cores. Second, the English voice produces 16~kHz audio and was played at 22.05~kHz, that is 38\% too fast and too high. Each sentence is now rendered completely and then played at the sample rate of its voice, with 150~ms of silence before it so that the sound device does not swallow the first syllable. Bangla is spoken with a Piper voice through Piper's Python package, which does not have the fault of the stand-alone program. On the laptop, a Bangla sentence of 1.5 to 4.3~s is rendered in 0.2 to 0.5~s once the voice is loaded (loading takes about 3~s and is done in the background at start-up). As a rough check of intelligibility, six Bangla sentences spoken by this voice were given to an online Bangla speech recogniser: two came back exactly, two with small differences (a numeral, a spelling), and two with wrong words. The new engine has not been heard or timed on the Pi yet; if rendering proves too slow there, one setting returns the glass to espeak-ng.

The glass was benchmarked again on its own board after the changes of 11 to 13 October, with 30 calls per row after 3 warm-up calls, and the text reader was timed through the call the application makes when ACTION is pressed (Table~\ref{tab:pi5_second}). The full report, its raw files and its scripts are in \texttt{benchmark\_report/}.

\textbf{The board was at its temperature limit.} The Pi was at 73~$^\circ$C before the first timed call, after two sessions with the application. It reached 88.7~$^\circ$C, and the firmware reported throttling or clock capping in 76\% of the samples during the text-reading test, with the CPU clock down to 1.5~GHz instead of 2.4~GHz. The power supply was not the cause (no under-voltage) and the fan was running. Four things in this run are findings about the software, not about the heat.

    \item \textbf{A short sign paid for the text detector twice.} The line-by-line path ran the detector to count the lines, and for fewer than four lines the normal path ran it again: 34.0~s instead of 18.3~s. The detector now runs once per capture and both paths use its boxes; the text read is identical to before on the benchmark images.
    \item \textbf{A picture with no text was not answered with ``no text found''.} After 27.5~s the fall-back reader for handwritten letters returned 48 letters that were not there. A picture in which the detector finds nothing is now answered at once, and the handwritten-letter reader is not tried on it.
    \item \textbf{The emotion figure of the first run was a frame without a face.} With a face to classify, one answer takes 680~ms, not 48~ms.
    \item \textbf{One pass of the text detector takes 11 to 18~s on this board}, with the full-precision ONNX detector and whatever is in the picture. Speech adds almost nothing to the response time (under 30~ms to synthesise a sentence). Not repeated in this run: the 30-minute sustained test, and a run on a cool board.

\section{First Field Test with the Glass}
\label{sec:field_test}
On 3~October 2026 the glass was tried by hand for the first time with its own camera, after the deployment problems of Section~\ref{sec:deploy_problems} had been solved and the application started correctly. This was an informal test by the developers, not a measurement, and no error rates were recorded. Three problems were observed:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Emotion detection did not work.} It was not in the glass application at all; it had existed only in the desktop harness. The mode was added the same day.
    \item \textbf{Currency recognition was unreliable.}
    \item \textbf{Bangla text was not read.}
\end{enumerate}
The cause of the second and third problem has not been established. The suspected causes are the 640$\times$480 frame and the fixed-focus lens of the camera, with which a note or a printed line held at reading distance may be neither large nor sharp enough; the training photographs and the benchmark images are sharp. A diagnosis script that compares the live 640$\times$480 frame with a high-resolution capture of the same scene is ready for the device; tuning the camera settings is the next step of the project.

On the laptop with a webcam at 1,280$\times$720, the same application was then run through all modes, with the live algorithm panel showing every model's output. Sound was at first missing on Windows and was fixed by a separate speech back-end. With the laptop webcam, notes held in the hand were recognised (Figure~\ref{fig:live_webcam}), which supports the suspicion that the problem on the glass lies in the picture from its camera and not in the detector.

\section*{Calculation Methods and Full Working}

This section presents the formulas used in this chapter and a worked example for each main table.

\subsection*{1) Detection Precision, Recall and mAP (Table~\ref{tab:det_overall})}
With $TP$, $FP$ and $FN$ the numbers of true positives, false positives and false negatives at a given IoU threshold,
\[
\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \qquad \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}, \qquad
\text{IoU}(A,B) = \frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}.
\]
The average precision (AP) of one class is the area under its precision--recall curve; mAP@0.5 is the mean AP over the 9 classes at IoU $\geq 0.5$, and mAP@0.5:0.95 is the mean over the ten IoU thresholds $0.50, 0.55, \dots, 0.95$.

\subsection*{2) Raw-Photograph Detection Rate and Top-1 Accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:wild})}
\[
\text{Detection rate} = \frac{\text{notes with at least one box}}{\text{all notes}} = \frac{450-7}{450} = \frac{443}{450} = 98.4\%,
\]
\[
\text{Top-1 accuracy} = \frac{\text{notes with correct top box}}{\text{all notes}} = \frac{436}{450} = 96.9\%,
\]
where $436 = 48+48+47+47+50+50+50+46+50$ is the sum of the correct notes over the nine denominations.

\subsection*{3) Authentication Accuracy and McNemar's Test (Table~\ref{tab:auth_views})}
With 208 test notes, one-view PRMVT accuracy $= 202/208 = 97.12\%$ and baseline accuracy $= 153/208 = 73.56\%$. McNemar's test uses only the discordant notes: $n_{01}$ (baseline correct, PRMVT wrong) and $n_{10}$ (baseline wrong, PRMVT correct):
\[
\chi^2 = \frac{(|n_{01}-n_{10}|-1)^2}{n_{01}+n_{10}},
\]
with one degree of freedom. For example, for the fixed-count fusion model at six views $n_{01}=3$ and $n_{10}=13$, giving $\chi^2 = 81/16 = 5.06$ and $p = 0.024$.

\subsection*{4) Expected Calibration Error}
Predictions are grouped into $M = 10$ confidence bins $B_m$:
\[
\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\bigl|\text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m)\bigr|.
\]

\subsection*{5) Emotion Macro-F1 (Table~\ref{tab:emotion_perclass})}
For each class $c$, $F1_c = 2P_cR_c/(P_c+R_c)$. For fear, $F1 = 2\times0.774\times0.554/(0.774+0.554) = 0.646$. Macro-F1 is the unweighted mean of the seven class F1 scores: $(0.936+0.839+0.799+0.870+0.646+0.627+0.851)/7 = 0.795$.

\subsection*{6) OCR Error Rates (Table~\ref{tab:ocr})}
\[
\text{CER} = \frac{S+D+I}{N},
\]
where $S$, $D$ and $I$ are the character substitutions, deletions and insertions needed to turn the recognised text into the ground truth, and $N$ is the number of ground-truth characters. WER is computed in the same way on words. Because insertions are counted, the rate can exceed 100\% when an engine returns much more text than the image contains, as for the ByT5 corrector in Table~\ref{tab:ocr_steps}.

\subsection*{7) Wilson Interval and the Safe Threshold (Table~\ref{tab:safe})}
For $x$ events in $n$ trials with $\hat{p} = x/n$ and $z = 1.96$, the Wilson interval is
\[
\frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}.
\]
For 0 counterfeits passed out of 88, the upper limit is $\frac{z^2/n}{1+z^2/n} = \frac{3.84/88}{1+3.84/88} = 4.2\%$. The threshold $\tau$ is the highest of the $n = 88$ validation counterfeit scores, so a new exchangeable counterfeit exceeds it with probability $1/(n+1) = 1/89 = 1.1\%$.


\section{Discussion}
The results answer the research questions of Section~\ref{sec:Research Question}.

\textbf{System (RQ1).} A five-mode assistive glass runs on a Raspberry Pi~5 within 1.5~GB of memory; in a second run after two sessions of use the board reached its temperature limit and was throttled (Section~\ref{sec:pi5_second}). Currency, objects, the watermark check and emotion respond in 50 to 330~ms; with speech, a currency answer takes under three seconds. Text reading is the slowest mode (about 19~seconds per capture, mostly in the text detector); for pages, a faster decoder and line-by-line reading are now in place.

\textbf{Synthetic data for currency (RQ2).} Training only on automatically labelled composites gave near-perfect scores on held-out composites and 91.5\% correct denominations on 1,536 independently collected photographs, though only 18.5\% on hand-held close-ups. Realistic backgrounds and physically motivated augmentation mattered more than the network architecture. 

\textbf{Number of views (RQ3).} A model trained with a fixed number of views fails when fewer are available, and unpredictably so across seeds; training on random view prefixes fixes this and keeps accuracy at about 98\% from one to six views. The controlled comparison shows that prefix sampling alone is responsible. The benefit depends on the fusion head and did not appear on every other dataset.

\textbf{Unseen prints (RQ4).} The usual JaalTaka split is a test of known reprints: a serial lookup scores 90.2\% on it, and frozen general-purpose backbones reach 98--99\%. On unseen prints accuracy falls by about 8 points. The watermark recovers about 5 of them and makes the result stable across seeds, but a properly fine-tuned ResNet-50 does as well. With about 24 test prints, the dataset cannot separate the two.

\textbf{Safety (RQ5).} A policy that never says ``counterfeit'' and confirms a note only above the highest validation counterfeit score passed none of 88 test counterfeits. It confirms 57.5\% of genuine notes from close-up views; on whole notes it mostly asks for a check by hand, so raising that confirmation rate is the next goal.

\textbf{Bangla OCR (RQ6).} Region selection matters most: choosing the main text block removed three quarters of the error. Parameter tuning and clean-up rules added small gains; pre-processing beyond contrast enhancement, other engines, other detectors, dictionaries, language models and multi-scale reading did not help. Fine-tuning the recogniser is a promising next step.

\textbf{Interaction (RQ7).} Separating capture from reading, cooldowns, queue clearing on mode change, mixed-script speech, immediate mode names and spoken capture guidance were designed for users without sight. Trial sessions with a visually impaired participant have started to measure how much they help.

\textbf{A general lesson.} The largest corrections in this project came from looking at the data and the test protocol and not from new models: the serial numbers on the counterfeit notes, the shape of the rendered Bangla text, the resolution of a cached image, the source of a ``wild'' test set. Each of these had produced a good-looking number that was wrong or meant something else.

\section{Scope of the Results}
The results above should be read with the following scope in mind; each point is also a direction for the next version (Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}):
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Glass camera.} The accuracy figures come from datasets and a laptop webcam; the camera settings of the worn glass are being tuned, and a test set taken through it is planned.
    \item \textbf{User study.} Trial sessions with a visually impaired participant have started; their results will be added when the survey is complete.
    \item \textbf{Counterfeit data.} The unseen-print results rest on about 24 distinct counterfeit prints of one currency; a larger print-disjoint collection would sharpen the comparison between methods.
    \item \textbf{Whole-note verdict.} On whole notes the safe policy mostly asks for a check by hand; the guided watermark check and a larger set of whole-note photographs are the way to confirm more genuine notes.
    \item \textbf{Text reading.} On real street photographs the letter error is about 39\% for Bangla and 62\% for Latin script, against 2.5\% on the rendered benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:ocr_real}), and reading is the slowest mode on the Pi.
    \item \textbf{UV check.} It accepts 85.6\% of the photographs of genuine notes and rejects about nine in ten simulated fibre-free sheets, but has not been tested on a real counterfeit note, on real printed paper under the lamp, or through the glass camera.
    \item \textbf{Objects.} With the model timed on the Pi (YOLOv8n) and small pictures, mAP@0.5 is 0.34 to 0.45; the classes are the 80 of COCO.
    \item \textbf{Other items.} Close-ups covered by fingers, objects outside the 80 COCO classes, a neural Bangla voice on the Pi, and battery life are left for future versions.
\end{enumerate}
