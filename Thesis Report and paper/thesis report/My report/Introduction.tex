\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
Vision is the sense through which people obtain most of the information about their surroundings. According to the World Health Organization, at least 2.2 billion people worldwide live with some form of near or distance vision impairment, and a large share of them live in low- and middle-income countries \cite{who2023vision, gbd2021vision}. For a person who is blind or has low vision, everyday actions that sighted people perform without thinking become difficult or impossible: reading a medicine label, a shop sign or a bus number, knowing what object is on the table, or checking which banknote is in the hand and whether it is genuine. Each of these tasks usually requires help from another person, which reduces independence, privacy and dignity.

Bangladesh faces this problem in a particular form. Most printed information in public places is written in Bangla, often mixed with English. Cash is still the main payment method in daily life, and the country has nine circulating denominations of Taka notes (2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1000). Counterfeit notes are a recurring problem in markets. At the same time, internet connectivity and the purchasing power of many visually impaired users are limited. A visually impaired person in Bangladesh therefore needs an assistive device that (i) speaks Bangla, (ii) recognises Bangladeshi currency, (iii) works without the internet, and (iv) is affordable and simple to operate.

Commercial assistive products such as OrCam MyEye \cite{orcam}, Envision Glasses \cite{envision} and Microsoft Seeing AI \cite{seeingai} show that camera-based assistance is technically possible. However, these products are expensive, are designed mostly for English and other major languages, rely in many functions on a smartphone or a cloud service, and do not support Bangladeshi Taka. Research prototypes built on Raspberry Pi boards \cite{readingaid_rpi2023, visionvoice2024, cameraocr2023} are cheaper, but most of them read only English text with Tesseract and send speech through online services such as Google Text-to-Speech.

This thesis presents \textbf{Savior Glass}, a wearable, offline, Bangla-first smart glass for visually impaired users. Savior Glass is designed around a Raspberry Pi~5 \cite{raspberrypi5}, a camera module mounted near the eyes, five physical push-buttons and an earphone or speaker. It offers five modes that a user cycles with a single button: \textit{text reading} (Bangla and English OCR), \textit{object detection} (spoken names of everyday objects in Bangla), \textit{currency detection} (denomination of Bangladeshi Taka notes, with a safe counterfeit check for 500 and 1,000 Taka notes), an optional \textit{online scene description}, and \textit{emotion} (the facial expression of the person in front of the user). All modes except the online one run on the device itself without internet access once the models are installed.

The work is also a data-engineering study. Public Bangladeshi currency datasets consist mainly of clean, close-up scans of notes. A model trained only on them learns that ``the whole frame is the note'' and fails on a live camera. We therefore built a copy-paste compositing pipeline that places notes on real photographs and generates exact bounding boxes automatically. For counterfeit detection we worked with a six-view genuine/counterfeit note dataset and found that a model trained only on six views collapses when it sees one view, which led to a prefix-robust training protocol. We then found that the dataset's usual split is easier than it looks: most counterfeit notes are copies of a few prints, and the same print appears in training and test. This led to a second split with unseen prints, to a check of the banknote watermark, and to a deliberately cautious spoken verdict.

The thesis is also an account of what did not work. The first Bangla OCR measurements used test images in which Bangla was drawn wrongly, and were redone. Several models that looked better on a test set were not adopted because the choice would have been made on test data. In the first hands-on test with the glass camera, currency recognition and Bangla reading did not work as they do in the laboratory. Throughout the thesis, every number is taken from a saved evaluation file, and results that are not yet measured, such as a study with visually impaired participants or battery life, are stated as not measured.


\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:Problem Statement}
Visually impaired people in Bangladesh depend on family members, shopkeepers or passers-by to read text, identify objects and handle money. This dependence creates three concrete risks: loss of independence in daily activities, exposure to fraud when receiving change or counterfeit notes, and loss of privacy when others must read personal documents. Existing assistive technologies do not solve these problems for Bangladeshi users.

Common limitations in current solutions include:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item High cost of commercial smart glasses and dependence on a smartphone or subscription;
    \item Dependence on cloud services for OCR, recognition or speech, which fails without internet;
    \item English-centric design, with little or no support for Bangla text and Bangla speech;
    \item No support for recognising Bangladeshi Taka, and almost no support for detecting counterfeit notes;
    \item Touch-screen or app-based interfaces that are hard to use without sight.
\end{itemize}

On the technical side, five specific problems had to be addressed:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Domain gap in currency recognition.} Models trained on clean note images do not transfer to real camera frames in which the note is small, rotated, partly lit and surrounded by clutter.
    \item \textbf{Unreliable number of views.} Counterfeit authentication needs several close views of a note, but a blind user cannot guarantee that all views are captured; a model that is accurate only with all six views is not usable.
    \item \textbf{Misleading evaluation.} Counterfeits printed from the same plate share one serial number. If such notes appear in both training and test data, a high score measures recognition of known reprints, not detection of new counterfeits.
    \item \textbf{The cost of a wrong verdict.} A wrong ``genuine'' costs the user money; a wrong ``counterfeit'' can lead to a public accusation. The device has to be safe before it is decisive.
    \item \textbf{Fragile Bangla reading on small hardware.} Bangla has conjuncts and vowel signs that move around the consonant, open OCR engines are weaker on it than on English, and everything has to run on a Raspberry Pi~5 without overheating.
\end{enumerate}

This project addresses these gaps and develops Savior Glass, an offline assistive smart glass that uniquely integrates:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item Bangla and English text reading with a capture-then-read interaction;
    \item Everyday object announcement with Bangla labels and repetition control;
    \item Detection of all nine Bangladeshi Taka denominations in cluttered scenes;
    \item Multi-view genuine/counterfeit authentication that stays accurate with fewer views;
    \item A watermark check with spoken capture guidance, and a verdict policy that never says ``counterfeit'';
    \item Offline Bangla/English speech, haptic feedback and physical buttons for control.
\end{itemize}


\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:Objective}

The primary objective of this project is to design, implement and evaluate Savior Glass, an affordable, offline, Bangla-first assistive smart glass for visually impaired people. The specific goals are:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item To build a wearable prototype on Raspberry Pi~5 that is operated entirely through physical buttons and audio feedback.
    \item To provide Bangla and English text reading using on-device OCR and offline text-to-speech.
    \item To announce everyday objects in front of the user in Bangla using a real-time object detector.
    \item To detect and name all nine denominations of Bangladeshi Taka in real-world scenes, by creating a large synthetic detection dataset without manual annotation.
    \item To develop a counterfeit-note authenticator that remains reliable when only one or a few views of the note are available.
    \item To measure counterfeit detection on prints never seen in training, and to design spoken feedback that is safe for a blind user.
    \item To improve Bangla text reading with a benchmark in which all choices are made on validation data.
    \item To add supporting features such as emotion announcement, haptic confirmation, field logging and a desktop test harness.
    \item To measure latency, memory and temperature of every mode on the Raspberry Pi~5.
    \item To evaluate every module with standard metrics on held-out data and to report the results and gaps honestly.
\end{enumerate}

Ultimately, the objective is to create a human-centred, low-cost and privacy-preserving assistive device that increases the independence of visually impaired people in Bangladesh.

\section{Scope of the Study}
\label{sec:Scope of the Study}

This research is concerned with the design, development and technical evaluation of a camera-based wearable assistant. The main functions of the system are the following:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Text Reading (OCR):} Recognition of printed Bangla and English text from the camera image and reading it aloud on request.

    \item \textbf{Object Detection:} Periodic detection of the 80 COCO object classes (person, chair, bottle, bus and so on) and announcement of the most relevant ones in Bangla.

    \item \textbf{Currency Detection:} Localisation and classification of the nine Bangladeshi Taka denominations, spoken in Bangla, with vibration confirmation.

    \item \textbf{Currency Authentication:} Classification of a 500 or 1,000 Taka note as genuine or counterfeit from one to six close-up views, a watermark check of a back-lit note, and a cautious spoken verdict on the glass.

    \item \textbf{Emotion:} Recognition of the facial expression of the person in front of the user, announced on request.

    \item \textbf{Online Scene Description:} An optional mode that sends one image to a cloud vision--language model; it needs the internet and an API key.
\end{itemize}

The study does not cover navigation, obstacle distance measurement, GPS, or voice-command control, although parts of these exist as experimental code. Model accuracy is evaluated on public datasets and on test sets built for this project; latency, memory and temperature are measured on the Raspberry Pi~5. A formal study with visually impaired participants, battery life and accuracy on photographs taken through the glass camera have not been measured and are presented as future work. The glass is an aid; it does not replace a white cane, a guide or a bank's counterfeit-detection equipment.


\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:Research Question}

This research addresses the following questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (system).} How can a multi-function assistive smart glass with Bangla support be designed and run on a Raspberry Pi~5, and what latency, memory and temperature does it reach?

    \item \textbf{RQ2 (synthetic data).} Can a detector trained on synthetically composited images, generated without manual annotation, recognise Bangladeshi Taka notes in independent real photographs?

    \item \textbf{RQ3 (number of views).} Why does a multi-view counterfeit authenticator trained on a fixed number of views fail when fewer views are available, and can a training protocol fix this?

    \item \textbf{RQ4 (unseen prints).} How much does counterfeit-detection accuracy fall when every test counterfeit comes from a print never seen in training, and can a physical security feature, the watermark, recover the loss?

    \item \textbf{RQ5 (safety).} Can the glass give counterfeit feedback that never accuses a genuine note and has a bounded chance of passing a counterfeit, and what does this safety cost in usefulness?

    \item \textbf{RQ6 (Bangla OCR).} Which parts of a Bangla OCR pipeline matter most for text read by a wearable camera: region selection, pre-processing, the engine, its parameters, post-processing or fine-tuning?

    \item \textbf{RQ7 (interaction).} How can the interaction (buttons, capture/read separation, spoken capture guidance, Bangla speech) be made suitable for users without sight? This question is answered only at the level of design; the user study that would test it has not been carried out.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
The main contributions of this thesis are:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item A complete five-mode assistive smart-glass system (Savior Glass) with Bangla-first speech and physical button control, measured on a Raspberry Pi~5: a median of 327~ms for the currency check, 1.48~GB peak memory and no thermal throttling in a 30-minute run.
    \item A copy-paste compositing pipeline that turns 5,073 clean note images into a 22,333-image detection dataset with automatic bounding boxes, and a YOLOv8s Taka detector trained on it that names 91.5\% of 1,536 independent photographs correctly.
    \item A prefix-robust multi-view training protocol that removes the train/test mismatch in the number of views: one-view accuracy 98.2\% against 71.2\% for fixed six-view training, over three seeds on a note-disjoint test set.
    \item An audit showing that most JaalTaka counterfeits share a few printed serial numbers, a print-disjoint split built from it, and the finding that accuracy drops from about 98\% to about 90\% on unseen prints.
    \item A watermark check with a learned localizer and spoken capture guidance that raises unseen-print accuracy to 95.0\%, and a verdict policy that never says ``counterfeit'' and passed 0 of 88 test counterfeits.
    \item A Bangla and English OCR benchmark with correctly shaped text and separate validation and test phrases and fonts, and an OCR pipeline that lowers the character error rate from 9.3\% to 2.4\%.
    \item Tools for the work that remains: a field logger, a print-locked data-collection tool, a pre-registered study protocol, a voice-driven questionnaire in Bangla, and an append-only database of all experiments.
    \item A file-backed evaluation of every module, including negative results, corrections of earlier figures and a list of measurements that are still missing.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Thesis Structure}

This thesis is organized into seven chapters, each addressing a specific aspect of the project. The chapters are outlined as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter 1: Introduction} \\
    This chapter provides the background, problem statement, objectives, scope, research questions, contributions and thesis structure.

    \item \textbf{Chapter 2: Literature Review} \\
    This chapter reviews assistive technologies for visually impaired people, smart glasses, OCR and text-to-speech systems, object detection, Bangladeshi currency recognition, multi-view learning, evaluation leakage and safe prediction.

    \item \textbf{Chapter 3: System Design and Methodology} \\
    This chapter describes the framework, hardware components, software design and the methodology of Savior Glass.

    \item \textbf{Chapter 4: Implementation Process} \\
    This chapter explains how each module was implemented, integrated and deployed.

    \item \textbf{Chapter 5: Dataset and Data Engineering} \\
    This chapter presents the datasets used, the synthetic compositing pipeline, the serial-number audit, the data splits and the OCR benchmark.

    \item \textbf{Chapter 6: Results and Discussion} \\
    This chapter reports the measured performance of each module on laptops and on the Raspberry Pi~5, the first field test, the negative results, and the limitations.

    \item \textbf{Chapter 7: Conclusion and Future Work} \\
    This chapter summarises the work, its social impact and the directions for future research.
\end{itemize}
